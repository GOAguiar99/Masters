\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

\autoref{chap:sysmodel} defined the crash-aware system conceptually, and \autoref{chap:implementation} mapped it onto the Veins/\gls{inet} artifact.
This chapter asks whether that realization works as intended: does crash-triggered Voice traffic obtain better wireless service than ordinary Best Effort under contention, and what cost does that protection impose on ordinary traffic?

The evaluation is a protection-versus-cost study, not a claim of deterministic real-time guarantees~\cite{kosekszott2012What,IEEE80211e_2005}.
Two highway-density packages are used: a light-density sensitivity sweep (10~vehicles) and a heavy-density congestion stress case (100~vehicles), with emphasis on the latter, which exposes the strongest separation~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Numeric bindings for timing, radio, load, and \gls{mac} policies are those of \autoref{tab:impl:parameters}; this chapter reports outcomes, not module wiring.

The chapter is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:setup} states the experimental matrix and seed budgets.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:metrics} defines metrics and aggregation.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:headline} states the headline answer and the two enabling conditions up front.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:light} explains, through the light and heavy uniform-load matrices, why Voice is policy-invariant there.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:hotspot} presents the hotspot regime where emergency protection recovers Voice service.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:cost} prices that protection on ordinary Best Effort traffic.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:discussion} discusses interpretation, practical significance, and limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:summary} summarizes the conditional takeaway.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

All experiments use the consolidated bindings in \autoref{tab:impl:parameters} and the reproducibility scripts in the public artifact~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Both density packages run the full matrix of five \gls{mac} policies and three offered-load profiles (15~configurations) with three OMNeT++ repetitions numbered \texttt{\#0}--\texttt{\#2}.
Each configuration name follows \texttt{<policy>\_netload\_<low|medium|high>} as documented in \autoref{chap:implementation}.
Raw \texttt{.sca}/\texttt{.vec} traces remain in the artifact clone (commit \texttt{5b0f584}); the compact per-run \gls{kpi} rows used to build the tables in this chapter are archived with the dissertation sources under \texttt{context/eval-kpis/}.
A second, 45-run matrix replays the same grid under a hotspot overlay that concentrates Best Effort airtime near the crash source (\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}); its rows are archived under \texttt{context/eval-kpis/highway\_heavy\_hotspot/}.

Contention is induced by offered load and fleet density rather than by synthetic random-waypoint mobility alone~\cite{veins_sommer2011}.
Best Effort inter-arrivals are Poisson: each vehicle redraws an exponential interval with the configured mean before every packet (\SI{500}{\milli\second}/\SI{250}{\milli\second}/\SI{125}{\milli\second} at low/medium/high).
At \texttt{netload\_high} that mean approaches 800~packets/s fleet-wide once all one hundred vehicles have entered; SUMO injects the fleet progressively, so the realized offer in these traces is about 22~thousand Best Effort transmissions per heavy run.
The crash source adds overlapping Voice repeats during the alert window (eight physical copies, \SI{5}{\milli\second} gap at high load), a sustained stream of roughly 400~physical packets/s while the window is active.
With a genuine Poisson offer the channel does \emph{not} enter queue overflow: Best Effort P95 stays sub-millisecond under plain \gls{dcf} and EDCA, and overflow counters remain zero.
The policy contrast at this load is therefore the Best Effort cost of adaptive blocking, not a Voice-tail race under saturation~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos,Continuous_Backoff_Freezing_Li}.

\section{Metrics and Aggregation}
\label{sec:eval:metrics}

The primary metrics are class-specific end-to-end delay, jitter, multicast reach, and \gls{mac}-layer losses; the definitions below apply to the Evaluation tables in this chapter.

End-to-end delay runs from packet creation to reception at each receiving vehicle.
Best Effort stamps creation at each physical send.
Crash Voice stamps creation at the logical burst start, so Voice delay is \emph{alert age}: time from the first attempt of a sequence until a receiver's first accepted copy, including any wait for a later physical repeat when earlier copies miss.
Mean delays aggregate per-run statistics across receivers, while tail and jitter statistics pool all receivers' per-packet samples, excluding locally looped-back copies.
The 95th percentile (\gls{p95}) is the main tail metric; mean absolute jitter summarizes short-term delay variation on the same samples.
A crash-node Best Effort slice records delay only for Best Effort packets whose source is the designated crash vehicle, so Voice can be compared with Best Effort from the same sender rather than with the fleet average.

Because every packet is multicast, reception is a fan-out that can exceed one:
Voice \emph{RX/alert} (equivalently RX per logical transmission in the load ladder) divides received alert copies by logical alert sequences;
Best Effort \emph{RX/TX} divides received background copies by transmitted packets.
\gls{mac} analysis reports total drops, drops per application transmission, and per-access-category attribution (Best Effort, Voice, unclassified); the Voice-attributed drop count therefore quantifies crash-frame loss at the \gls{mac} layer, summed over all nodes and including receive-side discards of corrupted frames.
When the per-category counters sum to twice the aggregate drop counter, the analysis pipeline halves the attributed counts so that categories sum to the aggregate (a double-counting guard in the parser); attribution is unavailable under \texttt{plain}, where no classifier assigns access categories.
Controller instrumentation adds Voice protection activations, Best Effort drops while blocked, and held Best Effort grants (\autoref{chap:implementation}).

Each repetition yields one run-level \gls{kpi} row.
Configuration summaries in this chapter are arithmetic means over seeds; tail statistics are computed per run over the pooled receiver samples and then averaged across seeds (a mean of per-run percentiles).
Headline delay, jitter, and reach tables also report the minimum--maximum range across those seeds.
Both densities use three seeds; \emph{no significance tests are reported}.
The numbers are therefore qualitative mechanism checks under a controlled matrix, not proven effect sizes~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.

The chapter is summarized, before the detailed matrices, by the headline section below; readers who need the short version may read \autoref{sec:eval:headline} and \autoref{sec:eval:summary} only.

\section{Voice Protection Under Load: The Headline Answer}
\label{sec:eval:headline}

Does crash-triggered Voice traffic obtain better wireless service than ordinary Best Effort under contention?
The answer from the full matrix is conditional, and the condition is the contribution: emergency preemption \emph{does} recover Voice service, but only when (i) near-crash airtime is concentrated in a few senders, and (ii) the alert burst cadence is dense enough to keep protection armed for most of the crash window.

\autoref{fig:eval:hotspot_dose} compresses that answer into one view.
Under hotspot contention (three bulk-uploading neighbors of the crash, \autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}), dense 20~ms bursts at \texttt{high} load see Voice P95 fall from 18.5~ms under plain \gls{dcf} to 14.6~ms under emergency ($-21\%$; mean: $-39\%$).
The same overlay at sparser alert cadences (\texttt{medium}, \texttt{low}) leaves emergency neutral or slightly harmful.
And under a uniform Poisson offer with no concentrated sender, \autoref{sec:eval:light} shows the complementary null result: every policy keeps Voice alert age within $0.08$~ms of plain \gls{dcf} at heavy high load, because the contention is diffuse and no suppression has a target.
Protection therefore behaves as an event-triggered escalation whose gain is set by channel state and burst cadence, not as an always-on Voice accelerator.

Sections~\ref{sec:eval:light}--\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot} substantiate these claims with the full matrices, and \autoref{sec:eval:cost} prices them: the same emergency preemption that buys the Voice tail trades away Best Effort queueing discipline, and bounded blocking without dropping (\texttt{stable}, \texttt{guarded}) buys nothing in either regime.

\section{Uniform Load: Why Voice Is Policy-Invariant}
\label{sec:eval:light}

The light-density sweep tests whether crash-aware protection is beneficial before the channel becomes saturated.
Voice P95 is set mainly by the crash-burst profile, not by the \gls{mac} policy: about 4.7~ms at \texttt{low}/\texttt{medium} (repeat gaps of 20~ms and 15~ms) and about 2.08~ms at \texttt{high} (5~ms gap), with seed ranges of a few hundred microseconds across all five policies.
The discriminative case is therefore Best Effort cost at \texttt{high} load with ten contending nodes.

\autoref{tab:eval:light} restates the same cells as percentages relative to \texttt{plain\_netload\_high}.
Positive percentages indicate degradation relative to that baseline; negative values indicate improvement.
\autoref{fig:eval:p95_gap_light} shows the Best Effort versus Voice P95 gap at the same density.

\autoref{tab:eval:light_abs}, \autoref{tab:eval:light}, and \autoref{fig:eval:p95_gap_light} reveal a consistent pattern: Voice alert age does not move with the policy, while untimely protection inflates Best Effort.
EDCA alone is indistinguishable from plain \gls{dcf} at this density.
\texttt{stable} raises Best Effort P95 from 0.44~ms to 49~ms; \texttt{guarded} to 9.0~ms.
\texttt{emergency} does not improve Voice, but it keeps Best Effort P95 at 0.37~ms by dropping stale Best Effort work instead of queueing it---the only policy whose Best Effort tail is \emph{shorter} than plain, at the cost of more \gls{mac} drops (\autoref{sec:eval:heavy:mac}).
The sweep is therefore a gating test: adaptive blocking without dropping is risky when protection windows can open under low channel stress.

The heavy-density package stresses the shared medium with one hundred vehicles on the same corridor and load matrix, and it reads the same way as the light sweep, only louder: Voice service stays policy-invariant and the loss mechanism explains why.
The subsections below collect that mechanistic evidence --- class-specific delay and jitter (\autoref{sec:eval:heavy:qos}), Voice loss with its drop attribution (\autoref{sec:eval:heavy:mac}), and reach invariance (\autoref{tab:eval:heavy_vo_rx}) --- while the policy cost these profiles impose on Best Effort is mapped separately in \autoref{sec:eval:cost}.

\autoref{tab:eval:heavy_qos} shows that Voice mean, P95, and jitter are the same to a few tens of microseconds across all five policies (P95 2.21--2.25~ms).
EDCA does not lower Voice alert age relative to plain \gls{dcf}, and emergency's 2.21~ms versus EDCA's 2.24~ms is a 1\% difference, not a tail-shaping win.
\autoref{fig:eval:p95_gap_heavy} and \autoref{fig:eval:vo_cdf} make that overlap explicit: the Voice distribution is the high-load burst schedule (8 repeats, 5~ms gap) waiting for a first successful copy.
The policy contrast is Best Effort.
\texttt{stable} raises fleet Best Effort P95 from 0.51~ms to 30~ms and jitter from 0.035~ms to 3.5~ms; \texttt{guarded} to 3.9~ms P95; emergency keeps fleet Best Effort P95 at 0.37~ms---below plain---because preemption drops deferred frames instead of serving them late.
Voice RX/alert is 9.95 for every policy (seed range 8.74--10.84, a geometry effect), while Best Effort RX/TX falls from 8.06 under \texttt{plain} to 6.93 under emergency.
The two ratios are not interchangeable: Voice divides received alert copies by logical alert sequences at a stopped crash node, whereas Best Effort divides received copies by physical transmissions averaged over the whole fleet, including the early sparse-injection phase (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:reach}).

\section{Hotspot Load: Where Protection Regains a Voice Effect}
\label{sec:eval:hotspot}

The null Voice effect of \autoref{sec:eval:light} has a structural cause: under a uniform Poisson offer, the contention that Voice faces is diffuse --- one hundred equiprobable senders each contribute about $1/100$ of the near-crash collision opportunities, so suppressing a node's own Best Effort queue for tens of milliseconds removes a negligible share of ambient airtime, and can only fire after a first copy was decoded.
The contraposition is testable --- if offered airtime is instead \emph{concentrated} in a few neighbors of the crash, suppression acquires a target and the Voice effect should reappear.

The hotspot overlay \texttt{\_hotspot3} (\autoref{sec:impl:matrix}) implements exactly that regime: the three vehicles entering the corridor right behind the crash node bulk-upload about \SI{2.4}{\mega\bit\per\second} each for the whole run, roughly tripling aggregate Best Effort transmissions per run from $22\times10^{3}$ to $72\times10^{3}$.
The crash source, the alert window, and every metric definition are unchanged; the matrix is five policies $\times$ three loads $\times$ three seeds named \texttt{<policy>\_hotspot\_<profile>}.

\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot} and \autoref{fig:eval:hotspot_delay} give the high-load cell.
Under hotspot contention the baselines finally degrade: plain \gls{dcf} and plain EDCA reach Voice P95 of 18.5--20~ms, up from 2.2~ms without the hotspot.
Emergency preemption holds the same tail at 14.6~ms and the Voice mean at 2.78~ms --- $-39\%$ against plain and $-38\%$ against EDCA on the mean, $-21\%/ -27\%$ on P95 --- and Voice-attributed collision losses fall from $25.2\times10^{3}$ to $17.8\times10^{3}$ ($-30\%$ versus EDCA), consistently across all three seeds (P95 ranges do not overlap the baselines').
The mechanism is visible in the counters: emergency's blocked duty cycle becomes near-continuous under dense bursts (sixteen thousand Best Effort frames dropped while blocked plus about one thousand suppressed grants), so the hot queues never refill the arena during the window.
Multicast reach, limited by highway geometry, stays at about ten receivers per alert for every policy: protection buys latency and integrity, not fan-out.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\subsection{Event-triggered escalation, not always-on QoS}
\label{sec:eval:disc:escalation}

The light-density sweep and the heavy-density load ladder jointly show that adaptive blocking is harmful when armed without Voice-limited congestion: \texttt{stable} and \texttt{guarded} inflate Best Effort delay while Voice alert age stays put.
In other words, the mechanism should be treated as an escalation level attached to genuine channel stress, not as an always-on replacement for ordinary EDCA marking~\cite{IEEE80211e_2005,aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Explicit DSCP-to-Voice mapping remains a reasonable default; bounded or emergency suppression should be reserved for periods when Best Effort competition is actually material to alert delivery---a regime the uniform Poisson matrix does not reach but the hotspot matrix does (\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}), which is exactly where emergency preemption flips sign from pure cost to measured Voice benefit (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).

\subsection{Reading the baseline correctly}
\label{sec:eval:disc:plain}

Two measurement traps deserve a single caution, because both are artifacts of aggregation rather than policy effects.
First, under \texttt{plain} both classes receive identical per-frame \gls{mac} treatment, yet \autoref{tab:eval:heavy_qos} shows a Voice--Best Effort delay gap (Voice P95 $\approx$ 2.2~ms versus fleet Best Effort 0.51~ms): the gap reflects different traffic processes, not a differentiating \gls{dcf} queue.
Best Effort pools one hundred senders over the whole~\SI{70}{\second} run, while Voice originates from one stopped crash node inside a~\SI{30}{\second} window and is timed from the logical burst start, so its first accepted copy typically waits one repeat gap.
Second, Voice RX/alert (9.95) exceeds Best Effort RX/TX (8.06 under \texttt{plain}) for structural reasons: Voice divides received copies by logical alert sequences whereas Best Effort divides by physical transmissions, and the stopped crash node sits in a denser neighborhood than the moving fleet average (\autoref{tab:eval:heavy_vo_rx}).
\label{sec:eval:disc:reach}
Cross-class comparisons within one policy therefore describe different traffic processes; the crash-node Best Effort slice is the controlled same-sender check, and cross-policy comparisons within one class remain the policy measurement.
Seeds also stay controlled: Poisson redraws keep Best Effort offer at about 22~thousand transmissions on every run, so seeds no longer split into an uncongested draw and two saturated draws.

\subsection{Regime-conditional significance of the Voice effects}
\label{sec:eval:disc:significance}

Under the uniform matrix the honest statement is that no Voice-tail reduction exists to claim: plain keeps Voice P95 near 2.2~ms at \texttt{netload\_high}, far below representative V2X latency budgets such as the~\SI{25}{\milli\second} end-to-end bound for cooperative platooning in \gls{threegpp} TS~22.186~\cite{ts22186}, and every alternative policy sits within tens of microseconds of it.
The hotspot study of \autoref{sec:eval:hotspot} shows the sign flip: when near-crash airtime is concentrated and bursts are dense, emergency preemption \emph{does} lower Voice delay on paper ($-39\%$ mean, $-21\%$ P95, $-30\%$ Voice-attributed collision losses versus \gls{edca} alone), with absolute P95 of 14.6~ms that re-enters the platooning budget band.
Three seeds per cell keep this a mechanism check rather than an effect-size estimate (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:limits}), but the boundary itself is the take-home: the same preemption is a cost center under uniform load and a Voice lever under concentrated load, so the stakes of the escalation question are regime-dependent, not stylistic.

\subsection{Protection cost, class granularity, and spatial fairness}
\label{sec:eval:disc:cost}

The clearest cost signal is that bounded protection makes Best Effort service worse than plain \gls{dcf} at high load (Best Effort P95 from 0.51~ms to 30~ms under \texttt{stable} and 3.9~ms under \texttt{guarded}), and \texttt{emergency} converts part of the backlog into explicit loss (about $2.5\times10^{3}$ Best Effort drops while blocked) while keeping the fleet Best Effort tail at 0.37~ms.
Because the model treats \emph{all} Best Effort traffic as suppressible, these penalties are a worst-case bound for the two-class abstraction (\autoref{sec:sys:scope}): in a real stack, traffic outside the current crash alert may still be safety relevant, so a binary Voice/Best Effort split can underestimate preemption harm.
Deployments would need finer-grained classes before comparable \gls{mac} aggression, and the trade-off is defensible only for genuinely critical, short-lived flows such as the alert window studied here.

\label{sec:eval:disc:spatial}
Nor is the penalty likely to be uniform across vehicles: protection is armed locally by overheard Voice frames, so vehicles near the crash source defer or drop Best Effort traffic while distant vehicles continue unaffected (\autoref{sec:sys:controller}).
The Best Effort cost is thus spatially concentrated around the event---arguably desirable for a crash---but pooled averages understate the worst-affected vehicles; per-vehicle fairness is left as future work.

\subsection{Preemption as a starvation cure under concentrated contention}
\label{sec:eval:disc:starvation}

The hotspot campaign surfaced a side effect not anticipated by the design: under concentrated contention, \gls{edca}-family queues starve the very node they should serve first.
At \texttt{hotspot\_high}, the crash node's own Best Effort packets that eventually reach receivers average $0.98$--$1.45$~\si{\second} of queueing delay under \gls{edca}, \texttt{stable}, and \texttt{guarded}, because the Best Effort access category rarely wins the local arbitration against continuous hotspot demand.
Emergency preemption drops that backlog while blocked, and the surviving Best Effort stream averages 3.3~ms --- closer to plain \gls{dcf} (1.5~ms) than to any deferring profile (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).
Dropping is therefore not only the mechanism that buys Voice service under a hot neighbor; it is also what keeps the alerted sender's own ordinary traffic responsive when the channel would otherwise be permanently lost to it.

\subsection{Burst cadence as an online arming trigger}
\label{sec:eval:disc:trigger}

The dose-response curve of \autoref{tab:eval:hotspot_dose} implies a measurable online signal for deciding when protection is worth its cost.
With a 20~ms burst period and 8-copy trains, block re-arming keeps the \gls{fsm} controller busy for most of the crash window, and preemption delivers its Voice gains; at 75~ms and 120~ms periods the blocked duty cycle falls to roughly one third or less, hot Best Effort refills the gaps, and the policy turns neutral-to-harmful.
An implementation could therefore arm emergency suppression only while recent alert cadence times the bounded block window (\autoref{tab:impl:parameters}) saturates the available blocking budget, releasing it otherwise: the controller already tracks exactly these quantities via its activation counter and \texttt{maxContinuousBlock} bookkeeping, so the trigger is an instrumentation problem, not a new measurement.
This closes the escalation design loop that \autoref{sec:eval:disc:escalation} opened, and is the concrete form of ``genuine congestion'' the deployment rules of \autoref{sec:eval:summary} require.

\subsection{Denial-of-service risk of Voice-triggered suppression}
\label{sec:eval:disc:dos}

Protection is armed by local Voice queue pressure and \emph{overheard} Voice frames without authenticating the sender, so a faulty or malicious node continuously emitting Voice-marked traffic could keep nearby vehicles in blocking mode, deferring or---under \texttt{emergency}---dropping their Best Effort traffic.
The risk is bounded but not removed: protection windows are finite (20--80~ms maximum continuous block plus a guard interval, \autoref{tab:impl:parameters}) and suppression affects only a node's own Best Effort transmissions, not reception.
Real deployment would additionally require V2X message authentication, rate limiting of alert bursts, and plausibility checks before Voice frames may trigger suppression.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:eval:disc:limits}

Heavy-density \glspl{kpi} average three seeds and report the min--max range on headline delay tables, still without confidence intervals or significance tests, so results are qualitative mechanism checks rather than proven effect sizes; all observed prioritization is statistical contention shaping, not deterministic real-time communication~\cite{kosekszott2012What}.
The evaluation is simulation-only, models a single crash source on a one-hop highway corridor, and uses the simplified \gls{phy} assumptions of \autoref{chap:implementation} and \autoref{sec:sys:scope}; concurrent alerts would arm overlapping protection windows whose interaction is not evaluated.
Results do not predict absolute performance under richer propagation or congestion control, and no adaptive-EDCA or \gls{dcc} baseline is implemented, so no superiority claim is made against those families~\cite{etsi_dcc_2018}.
The configured \gls{edca} parameters follow the IEEE~802.11 BSS QoS defaults rather than the distinct \gls{ocb} default set, and group-addressed frames use \gls{inet}'s default fastest-mandatory-mode rate selection rather than a basic-rate policy (\autoref{sec:impl:radio}); both choices are uniform across policies but may shift absolute values relative to deployed 802.11p stacks.
The \texttt{high} crash-burst profile is a deliberate stress case rather than a standardized alert schedule, and no application-level safety deadline is evaluated.
The three adaptive profiles are fixed design points whose triggers are not individually ablated, although their spread of block windows (4--15~ms) and Voice thresholds (1--3 packets) acts as a coarse sensitivity ladder; a systematic parameter sweep is future work.
The hotspot cells (\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}) place the streaming nodes by injection order rather than by a received-power map, so the exact gain magnitudes depend on that geometry; the sign of the effect --- and its absence at sparser burst cadences --- is the claim, not the decimals.
Within that scope the comparison remains internally fair: all configurations share the same \gls{phy} stack, mobility trace, and load matrix, isolating \gls{mac}-policy effects~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:eval:summary}

The evaluation compared five \gls{mac} policies on the same highway matrix and found a regime-conditional answer rather than a universal Voice-tail race.
The headline result is positive but bounded: emergency preemption recovers Voice service \emph{only} when near-crash airtime is concentrated in a few senders \emph{and} bursts are dense enough to keep protection armed --- at \texttt{hotspot\_high} it cuts Voice mean alert age by $39\%$ and P95 by $21\%$ relative to plain \gls{dcf}, while the same overlay at sparser cadences, and uniform Poisson load at any cadence, leaves every policy within a few tens of microseconds of the baseline (Voice P95 $\approx$ 2.2~ms at heavy \texttt{netload\_high}).
The flat sections explain the boundary mechanistically: Voice loss is receiver-side collision corruption, reach is geometry-limited at about ten receivers per alert, and diffuse one-hundred-sender contention gives node-local suppression no target.
The cost side is equally clear: bounded blocking without dropping (\texttt{stable}, \texttt{guarded}) only taxes Best Effort (fleet P95 up to 30~ms and 3.9~ms), while emergency converts the backlog into about $2.5\times10^{3}$ explicit drops and keeps both fleet and crash-sender tails below plain.
The practical takeaway is therefore conditional: explicit DSCP-to-Voice marking is a reasonable default; adaptive blocking---especially emergency preemption---is justified as an event-triggered escalation where near-crash congestion is concentrated, with authentication and rate limiting as deployment prerequisites.
The absolute Voice delays in this synthetic setting remain far below representative platooning budgets~\cite{ts22186}.

The subsequent chapter concludes the dissertation by summarizing these findings, stating the conditional practical takeaway, and outlining limitations and future work.
