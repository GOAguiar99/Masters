\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

\autoref{chap:sysmodel} defined the crash-aware system conceptually, and \autoref{chap:implementation} mapped it onto the Veins/\gls{inet} artifact.
This chapter asks whether that realization works as intended: does crash-triggered Voice traffic obtain better wireless service than ordinary Best Effort under contention, and what cost does that protection impose on ordinary traffic?

The evaluation measures how much protection each policy gives to Voice traffic and how much that protection costs Best Effort traffic; it makes no claim of deterministic real-time guarantees.
Two highway densities are used: a light density of 10~vehicles, which tests sensitivity, and a heavy density of 100~vehicles, which stresses the channel and receives most of the attention~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
The timing, radio, load, and \gls{mac}-policy parameters are those of \autoref{tab:impl:parameters}, so this chapter reports results only.

The chapter is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:setup} states the experimental matrix and seed budgets.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:metrics} defines metrics and aggregation.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:headline} gives an overview of the results and of the two conditions under which protection helps Voice traffic.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:hotspot} presents the main positive result, the Voice improvement under concentrated near-crash load.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:light} shows that the policies do not change Voice service under uniform load.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:cost} quantifies the cost of protection to Best Effort traffic.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:discussion} discusses interpretation, practical significance, and limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:summary} summarizes the chapter.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

All experiments use the consolidated bindings in \autoref{tab:impl:parameters} and the reproducibility scripts in the public artifact~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Both density packages run the full matrix of five \gls{mac} policies and three offered-load profiles (15~configurations) with three OMNeT++ repetitions numbered \texttt{\#0}--\texttt{\#2}.
Each configuration name follows \texttt{<policy>\_netload\_<low|medium|high>} as documented in \autoref{chap:implementation}.
Simulation outputs and the scripts that regenerate the tabulated \glspl{kpi} are released with the public artifact~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
A second matrix replays the same fifteen configurations under a hotspot overlay that concentrates Best Effort airtime near the crash source (15~configurations $\times$ 3~seeds $=$ 45~seeded runs; \autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}).

Contention is induced by offered load and fleet density rather than by synthetic random-waypoint mobility alone~\cite{veins_sommer2011}.
Best Effort inter-arrivals are Poisson: each vehicle redraws an exponential interval with the configured mean before every packet (\SI{500}{\milli\second}/\SI{250}{\milli\second}/\SI{125}{\milli\second} at low/medium/high).
At \texttt{netload\_high} that mean approaches 800~packets/s fleet-wide once all one hundred vehicles have entered; SUMO injects the fleet progressively, so the realized offer in these traces is about 22~thousand Best Effort transmissions per heavy run.
The crash source adds overlapping Voice repeats during the alert window (eight physical copies, \SI{5}{\milli\second} gap at high load), a sustained stream of roughly 400~physical packets/s while the window is active.
With a genuine Poisson offer the channel does \emph{not} enter queue overflow: Best Effort P95 stays sub-millisecond under plain \gls{dcf} and EDCA, and overflow counters remain zero.
Under this uniform offer, the policies therefore differ mainly in their cost to Best Effort traffic~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos,Continuous_Backoff_Freezing_Li}.

\autoref{tab:eval:decisions} summarizes the main experimental decisions of this section and the reason for each.
The detailed account remains in the text above and in \autoref{chap:implementation}.

\section{Metrics and Aggregation}
\label{sec:eval:metrics}

The primary metrics are class-specific end-to-end delay, jitter, multicast reach, and \gls{mac}-layer losses; the definitions below apply to the Evaluation tables in this chapter.

End-to-end delay runs from packet creation to reception at each receiving vehicle.
Best Effort stamps creation at each physical send.
Crash Voice stamps creation at the logical burst start, so Voice delay is \emph{alert age}: time from the first attempt of a sequence until a receiver's first accepted copy, including any wait for a later physical repeat when earlier copies miss.
Mean delays aggregate per-run statistics across receivers, while tail and jitter statistics pool all receivers' per-packet samples, excluding locally looped-back copies.
The \gls{p95} is the main tail metric; mean absolute jitter summarizes short-term delay variation on the same samples.
A crash-node Best Effort slice records delay only for Best Effort packets whose source is the designated crash vehicle, so Voice can be compared with Best Effort from the same sender rather than with the fleet average.

Because every packet is multicast, reception is a fan-out that can exceed one:
Voice \emph{RX/alert} divides received alert copies by logical alert sequences;
Best Effort \emph{RX/TX} divides received background copies by transmitted packets.
\gls{mac} analysis reports total drops, drops per application transmission, and per-access-category attribution (Best Effort, Voice, unclassified); the Voice-attributed drop count quantifies crash-frame loss at the \gls{mac} layer, summed over all nodes and including receive-side discards of corrupted frames.
When the per-category counters sum to twice the aggregate drop counter, the analysis pipeline halves the attributed counts so that categories sum to the aggregate (a double-counting guard in the parser); attribution is unavailable under \texttt{plain}, where no classifier assigns access categories.
Controller instrumentation adds Voice protection activations, Best Effort drops while blocked, and held Best Effort grants (\autoref{chap:implementation}).

Each repetition yields one run-level \gls{kpi} row.
Configuration summaries in this chapter are arithmetic means over seeds; tail statistics are computed per run over the pooled receiver samples and then averaged across seeds (a mean of per-run percentiles).
The main delay, jitter, and reach tables also report the minimum--maximum range across those seeds.
Both densities use three seeds; \emph{no significance tests are reported}.
The numbers are therefore qualitative mechanism checks under a controlled matrix, not proven effect sizes~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Unless noted otherwise, relative improvements are versus \texttt{plain}; Voice-attributed \gls{mac}-drop comparisons use EDCA because \texttt{plain} has no access-category attribution.

\section{Overview of the Results}
\label{sec:eval:headline}

Across the full matrix, the protection policies improve Voice service only under specific conditions: emergency preemption reduces Voice delay when (i)~a few senders near the crash dominate the channel and (ii)~the alert bursts are frequent enough to keep protection active for most of the crash window.
\autoref{tab:eval:glance} summarizes the main results, and \autoref{fig:eval:regime_contrast} compares Voice P95 under the two load patterns.

At heavy \texttt{netload\_high}, every policy reaches a Voice P95 near 2.2~ms.
This floor comes from the \SI{2}{\milli\second} dispatch jitter that the alert generator applies to each copy, and not from channel access; with the \SI{5}{\milli\second} jitter of the low and medium profiles, the floor is near 4.6--4.9~ms (\autoref{sec:eval:light}).
Under \texttt{hotspot\_high}, the Voice tail grows, and only emergency reduces it.
Protection is therefore useful as an escalation step triggered by the crash event, and its benefit depends on where the load is concentrated and how often alerts are sent; enabling it permanently does not speed up Voice traffic.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:hotspot} details the hotspot results, Section~\ref{sec:eval:light} the uniform-load results, and Section~\ref{sec:eval:cost} the cost to Best Effort traffic.

\section{Voice Service Under Concentrated Load}
\label{sec:eval:hotspot}

Under a uniform Poisson offer, the frames that can collide with an alert come from about one hundred senders.
Suppressing Best Effort traffic at one node therefore removes only a negligible share of the channel load, and the suppression can start only after a first Voice copy has been decoded (\autoref{sec:eval:light}).
The situation changes when a few vehicles next to the crash dominate the channel: suppression at those vehicles then removes most of the competing load, and a Voice effect should appear.

The hotspot overlay \texttt{\_hotspot3} (\autoref{sec:impl:matrix}) implements this situation.
The three vehicles entering the corridor immediately after the crash node bulk-upload about \SI{2.4}{\mega\bit\per\second} each for the whole run, roughly tripling aggregate Best Effort transmissions per run from $22\times10^{3}$ to $72\times10^{3}$.
That higher offer pushes Voice-attributed \gls{mac} losses from about $3.7\times10^{3}$ under uniform \texttt{netload\_high} to about $25\times10^{3}$ under hotspot EDCA, before the adaptive policies are considered.
The crash source, the alert window, and every metric definition are unchanged; configurations are named \texttt{<policy>\_hotspot\_<profile>}.

\subsection{High-load results}
\label{sec:eval:hotspot:high}

\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot} and \autoref{fig:eval:hotspot_delay} report the high-load hotspot configuration, which has the densest alert bursts and is the only hotspot configuration in which Voice delay depends on the policy.
Plain \gls{dcf} and plain EDCA reach Voice P95 of 18.5--20~ms.
Emergency holds the same tail at 14.6~ms and the Voice mean at 2.78~ms ($-39\%$ mean and $-21\%$ P95 versus plain; $-38\%$ / $-27\%$ versus EDCA).
Voice-attributed collision losses fall from $25.2\times10^{3}$ to $17.8\times10^{3}$ ($-30\%$ versus EDCA), and the three-seed P95 ranges do not overlap the baselines'.
Emergency lowers both central tendency and tail, but P95 remains an order of magnitude above the mean because alert age is dominated by occasional late first-copy successes under collision-heavy windows.
The controller counters show how this happens.
With eight copies every 20~ms, the nodes stay blocked almost continuously, and about $16.1\times10^{3}$ Best Effort frames are dropped while blocked (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).
The queues of the heavy senders therefore cannot refill the channel during the alert window.
Multicast reach stays at about ten receivers per alert for every policy, limited by geometry: protection reduces delay and losses but does not reach more vehicles.

Under concentrated load, the step that matters is dropping Best Effort frames; deferring them is not enough.
\texttt{stable} and \texttt{guarded} leave Voice P95 at the EDCA level (20--21~ms), because blocking without dropping never empties the queues of the heavy senders.
Like EDCA, these two policies also starve the crash node's own Best Effort traffic: its delivered packets wait $0.98$--$1.45$~\si{\second} on average, whereas emergency and plain keep this delay at 3.3~ms and 1.5~ms respectively (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).
Preemption thus has a second benefit for the crash node: besides improving Voice service, it keeps the node's ordinary traffic flowing when the continuous hotspot demand would otherwise hold the channel.

\subsection{Effect of the alert burst cadence}
\label{sec:eval:hotspot:dose}

The Voice gain is conditional on burst density (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot_dose}, \autoref{fig:eval:hotspot_dose}).
At \texttt{hotspot\_high} the 20~ms period with eight copies keeps protection active for most of the crash window.
At \texttt{medium} (75~ms, four copies) and \texttt{low} (120~ms, three copies) the blocked duty cycle falls to roughly one third or less, the heavy senders refill the gaps between blocks, and emergency no longer helps: it increases the Voice mean by $10\%$ and $16\%$ respectively.
Voice service therefore improves only when both conditions hold: the near-crash load is concentrated, and the alerts are frequent enough to keep protection active.

\section{Voice Service Under Uniform Load}
\label{sec:eval:light}

The uniform Poisson matrix is the reference case for \autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}.
Without a concentrated near-crash sender, every policy keeps Voice alert age within a few tens of microseconds of plain \gls{dcf}, and the policies differ only in their cost to Best Effort traffic.

\subsection{Light density}
\label{sec:eval:light:gating}

\autoref{tab:eval:light} reports light-density high-load cells versus \texttt{plain\_netload\_high}.
Voice stays flat near 2.08~ms P95 (within $1.3\%$ of plain).
\texttt{stable} and \texttt{guarded} inflate Best Effort P95 from 0.44~ms to 49~ms and 9.0~ms; emergency shortens it to 0.37~ms by dropping queued Best Effort frames, which does not affect Voice.
The light density is therefore a first check: even with little channel stress, protection windows open, and blocking without dropping already harms Best Effort traffic.

\subsection{Heavy density}
\label{sec:eval:heavy:qos}

At one hundred vehicles the policies again leave Voice service unchanged.
\autoref{tab:eval:heavy_qos} and \autoref{fig:eval:p95_gap_heavy} show Voice mean, P95, and jitter within a few tens of microseconds across all five policies (P95 2.21--2.25~ms).
EDCA does not lower Voice alert age relative to plain \gls{dcf}.
The policy contrast is Best Effort: \texttt{stable} raises fleet Best Effort P95 from 0.51~ms to 30~ms; \texttt{guarded} to 3.9~ms; emergency keeps 0.37~ms by preemption.
That lower emergency P95 is not better Best Effort service: delay is measured only on delivered packets, so dropping frames while blocked removes the slow tail from the sample while the cost appears as reach loss (Best Effort RX/TX falls from 8.06 under \texttt{plain} to 6.93).
Voice RX/alert is 9.95 for every policy (geometry-limited fan-out).
Voice-attributed \gls{mac} drops sit near $3.7\times10^{3}$ under every QoS-enabled policy and do not separate emergency from EDCA; Best Effort queue overflow is zero throughout.
Under diffuse contention, suppression at each node therefore cannot improve Voice service: it changes neither how many alerts arrive nor how many collide.

The floor that every policy shares is set by the alert generator itself rather than by channel access.
Each physical copy of a burst, including the first, is dispatched at an offset $\max(0,\,U(-J,\,J))$ after the logical burst timestamp that defines the delay origin, where $J$ is the configured repeat jitter (\autoref{sec:impl:crash}).
That offset therefore has a point mass at zero and a 95th percentile of $0.9\,J$: about \SI{1.8}{\milli\second} at the high-load setting $J=\SI{2}{\milli\second}$ and about \SI{4.5}{\milli\second} at the low and medium setting $J=\SI{5}{\milli\second}$.
Adding the sub-millisecond transfer delay measured on the same samples reproduces the observed floors of about 2.2~ms and 4.6--4.9~ms respectively, and the mean behaves the same way: $\E[\max(0,\,U(-2,2))]=\SI{0.5}{\milli\second}$ accounts for half of the 0.99~ms Voice mean in \autoref{tab:eval:heavy_qos}.
Only when the first copy is lost does the inter-copy gap~$g$ enter, adding one further quantum per missed repeat; robust vehicular broadcast schemes rely on this kind of application-level repetition~\cite{Robust_Broadcast_Ma,Reliable_Broadcast_Farnoud}.
At the 95th percentile under diffuse load such losses are rare, so contention changes the delay distribution only slightly above the floor set by the generator.
The 2.2~ms figure should therefore be read with two caveats: it is not a pure medium-access measurement, and no MAC policy could have changed it.

\section{Cost to Best Effort Traffic}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

Section~\ref{sec:eval:hotspot} showed what emergency preemption gains under concentrated load.
This section quantifies what protection costs, first under concentrated load and then under uniform load, where it brings no Voice benefit.

\subsection{Cost under concentrated load}
\label{sec:eval:cost:hotspot}

At \texttt{hotspot\_high}, the Voice improvement cannot be separated from its Best Effort cost.
Emergency drops about $16.1\times10^{3}$ Best Effort frames while blocked (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).
That preemption is also what prevents crash-node Best Effort starvation: under EDCA/\texttt{stable}/\texttt{guarded} the same-sender Best Effort mean reaches $0.98$--$1.45$~\si{\second}, versus 3.3~ms under emergency.
\autoref{fig:eval:gain_cost} places the tradeoff in $\Delta$-space versus plain \gls{dcf}: under hotspot high load, emergency alone moves left on the Voice axis; under uniform high load, the adaptive policies do not reduce Voice P95, while Best Effort P95 rises, most of all under \texttt{stable}.

\subsection{Cost under uniform load}
\label{sec:eval:heavy:ladder}

Under the uniform matrix, the controller still activates protection, but without any Voice benefit (\autoref{tab:eval:heavy_p95}, \autoref{tab:eval:heavy_ctrl}, \autoref{fig:eval:ctrl}).
\texttt{stable} and \texttt{guarded} accumulate about $1.1\times10^{5}$ protection activations per high-load run with zero preemption counters, stretching fleet Best Effort P95 to 30~ms and 3.9~ms.
Emergency drops about $2.5\times10^{3}$ Best Effort frames while blocked and suppresses twelve grants; it keeps the fleet and crash-sender Best Effort tails at 0.37~ms and 0.44~ms, below plain, while Voice P95 remains at the 2.2~ms floor.
In summary, deferring Best Effort frames adds delay and dropping them removes traffic; dropping improves Voice service only when the load near the crash is concentrated (\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

The results show that the trade-off between protection and cost depends on the load pattern. This section discusses when the adaptive profiles help, what they cost, and how the measurement method and deployment constraints limit these conclusions.

\subsection{When to enable adaptive protection}
\label{sec:eval:disc:escalation}

Adaptive blocking is harmful when it is active while congestion does not limit Voice service: \texttt{stable} and \texttt{guarded} inflate Best Effort delay while Voice alert age stays the same (\autoref{sec:eval:light}).
The mechanism should therefore be enabled as an escalation step when the channel is genuinely stressed, and should not permanently replace ordinary EDCA marking~\cite{IEEE80211e_2005,aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Explicit DSCP-to-Voice mapping remains a reasonable default; bounded or emergency suppression should be reserved for the concentrated regime of \autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}, the only case in which preemption produces a measured Voice benefit instead of only a cost (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).

\subsection{Interpretation of the baseline}
\label{sec:eval:disc:plain}

Two differences in the results come from how the data are aggregated and not from the policies.
First, under \texttt{plain} both classes receive identical per-frame \gls{mac} treatment, yet \autoref{tab:eval:heavy_qos} shows a Voice--Best Effort delay gap: the gap reflects different traffic processes (one crash-node alert stream versus a fleet-wide Poisson offer), not a differentiating \gls{dcf} queue.
Second, Voice RX/alert exceeds Best Effort RX/TX for structural reasons: the two ratios have different denominators, and the stopped crash node has a denser neighborhood.
Cross-class comparisons within one policy therefore describe different traffic processes; the crash-node Best Effort slice is the controlled same-sender check, and cross-policy comparisons within one class remain the policy measurement.
Because inter-arrivals are redrawn from a Poisson process, every uniform run offers about 22~thousand Best Effort transmissions, so the three seeds face comparable congestion.

\subsection{Practical significance of the Voice results}
\label{sec:eval:disc:significance}

Under the uniform matrix there is no Voice-tail reduction to claim against representative V2X latency budgets such as the~\SI{25}{\milli\second} end-to-end bound for cooperative platooning in \gls{threegpp} TS~22.186~\cite{ts22186}: absolute Voice P95 stays near 2.2~ms for every policy (\autoref{sec:eval:light}).
Part of that margin is also structural and does not come from the channel, since about \SI{1.8}{\milli\second} of the 2.2~ms is the alert generator's own per-copy dispatch jitter (\autoref{sec:eval:light}); the medium-access contribution to the uniform-load tail is smaller still.
The hotspot configuration of \autoref{tab:eval:hotspot} is where absolute tails approach that budget and where emergency reduces them.
With three seeds per configuration, this remains a check of the mechanism rather than an estimate of effect size (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:limits}). The main result is the boundary itself: the same preemption only costs Best Effort traffic under uniform load, and it improves Voice service under concentrated load.

\subsection{Protection cost, class granularity, and spatial fairness}
\label{sec:eval:disc:cost}

Bounded protection makes Best Effort service worse than plain \gls{dcf} at high uniform load, and emergency converts part of the backlog into explicit loss while keeping the fleet Best Effort tail short (\autoref{sec:eval:cost}).
Because the model treats \emph{all} Best Effort traffic as suppressible, these penalties are a worst-case bound for the two-class abstraction (\autoref{sec:sys:scope}): in a real stack, traffic outside the current crash alert may still be safety relevant.
Deployments would need finer-grained classes before comparable \gls{mac} aggression, and the trade-off is defensible only for genuinely critical, short-lived flows such as the alert window studied here.

\label{sec:eval:disc:spatial}
Nor is the penalty uniform across vehicles: protection is activated locally by overheard Voice frames, so vehicles near the crash source defer or drop Best Effort while distant vehicles continue unaffected (\autoref{sec:sys:controller}).
The Best Effort cost is therefore concentrated around the event, which is arguably desirable for a crash, but pooled averages understate the cost for the most affected vehicles; per-vehicle fairness is left as future work.

\subsection{Best Effort starvation at the crash node}
\label{sec:eval:disc:starvation}

Under concentrated contention, \gls{edca}-based policies can starve the Best Effort traffic of the crash node itself.
At \texttt{hotspot\_high}, crash-node Best Effort means of $0.98$--$1.45$~\si{\second} under EDCA/\texttt{stable}/\texttt{guarded} collapse to 3.3~ms under emergency (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).
Dropping Best Effort frames therefore both improves Voice service and prevents this starvation when continuous hotspot demand would otherwise hold the channel.

\subsection{Burst cadence as a trigger for protection}
\label{sec:eval:disc:trigger}

The dose-response of \autoref{tab:eval:hotspot_dose} implies a measurable online signal for deciding when protection is worth its cost: enable emergency suppression only while the recent alert rate multiplied by the bounded block window (\autoref{tab:impl:parameters}) fills the available blocking budget.
The controller already tracks the activation count and \texttt{maxContinuousBlock}, so this trigger requires only additional instrumentation.
Such a trigger would implement the escalation step proposed in \autoref{sec:eval:disc:escalation}.

\subsection{Denial-of-service risk of Voice-triggered suppression}
\label{sec:eval:disc:dos}

Protection is activated by local Voice queue pressure and \emph{overheard} Voice frames without authenticating the sender, so a faulty or malicious node continuously emitting Voice-marked traffic could keep nearby vehicles in blocking mode.
The risk is bounded but not removed: protection windows are finite (20--80~ms maximum continuous block plus a guard interval, \autoref{tab:impl:parameters}) and suppression affects only a node's own Best Effort transmissions.
Real deployment would additionally require V2X message authentication, rate limiting of alert bursts, and plausibility checks before Voice frames may trigger suppression.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:eval:disc:limits}

Heavy-density \glspl{kpi} average three seeds and report the min--max range in the main delay tables, still without confidence intervals or significance tests, so results are qualitative mechanism checks rather than proven effect sizes; all observed prioritization is statistical contention shaping, not deterministic real-time communication~\cite{kosekszott2012What}.
The evaluation is simulation-only, models a single crash source on a one-hop highway corridor, and uses the simplified \gls{phy} assumptions of \autoref{chap:implementation} and \autoref{sec:sys:scope}; concurrent alerts would arm overlapping protection windows whose interaction is not evaluated.
Results do not predict absolute performance under richer propagation or congestion control, and no adaptive-EDCA or \gls{dcc} baseline is implemented, so no superiority claim is made against those families~\cite{etsi_dcc_2018}.
The configured \gls{edca} parameters follow the IEEE~802.11 BSS QoS defaults rather than the distinct \gls{ocb} default set, and group-addressed frames use \gls{inet}'s default fastest-mandatory-mode rate selection rather than a basic-rate policy (\autoref{sec:impl:radio}); both choices are uniform across policies but may shift absolute values relative to deployed 802.11p stacks.
The \texttt{high} crash-burst profile is a deliberate stress case rather than a standardized alert schedule, and no application-level safety deadline is evaluated.
The three adaptive profiles are fixed design points whose triggers are not individually ablated, although their spread of block windows (4--15~ms) and Voice thresholds (1--3 packets) acts as a coarse sensitivity ladder; a systematic parameter sweep is future work.
The hotspot cells place streaming nodes by injection order rather than by a received-power map, so the exact gain magnitudes depend on that geometry; the claim is the direction of the effect and its absence at sparser burst cadences, not the exact values.
Within that scope the comparison remains internally fair: all configurations share the same \gls{phy} stack, mobility trace, and load matrix, isolating \gls{mac}-policy effects~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval:summary}

The evaluation compared five \gls{mac} policies on the same highway matrix and found that the answer depends on the load pattern.
The main positive result comes from \texttt{hotspot\_high}: emergency preemption improves Voice service when the near-crash load is concentrated and the bursts are frequent enough to keep protection active (\autoref{tab:eval:hotspot}).
Under the uniform Poisson matrix, Voice alert age stays at the generator's dispatch-jitter floor for every policy, while adaptive blocking mainly increases Best Effort delay (\autoref{sec:eval:light}).
Bounded blocking without dropping (\texttt{stable}, \texttt{guarded}) never converts hotspot stress into Voice service; dropping Best Effort frames is the step that both reduces the Voice tail and prevents Best Effort starvation at the crash node.
In practice, explicit DSCP-to-Voice marking is a reasonable default, while adaptive blocking, especially emergency preemption, is justified as an escalation step triggered by the crash event where near-crash congestion is concentrated; authentication and rate limiting are prerequisites for deploying it.

\autoref{chap:conclusion} summarizes these findings, states their practical implications, and outlines limitations and future work.
