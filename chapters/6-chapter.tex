\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

The main objective of this dissertation was to evaluate whether crash-triggered Voice traffic can obtain better wireless service than ordinary Best Effort traffic in decentralized vehicular Wi-Fi, using only local, standard-compliant \gls{mac} mechanisms, and to quantify the cost of that protection.
The study used a two-class traffic model. All vehicles exchange periodic Best Effort multicast, one designated crash source injects temporary Voice alert bursts marked DSCP~46, and five \gls{mac} policies are compared on the same highway matrix: plain \gls{dcf}, EDCA with explicit Voice mapping, and three adaptive extensions (\texttt{stable}, \texttt{guarded}, \texttt{emergency}) that suppress competing Best Effort while alerts are active~(\autoref{chap:sysmodel}, \autoref{chap:implementation}).
The evaluation is simulation-only and mechanism-oriented: it isolates statistical contention shaping under controlled load and density, not deterministic real-time guarantees or application-level crash outcomes (\autoref{chap:evaluation}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Main Findings}
\label{sec:conc:findings}

The main positive result comes from the hotspot configuration (\autoref{tab:eval:glance}, \autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}). With three crash-adjacent vehicles bulk-uploading about \SI{2.4}{\mega\bit\per\second} each and the dense 20~ms burst profile, emergency preemption improves Voice service, with $39\%$ lower mean and $21\%$ lower P95 alert age than plain \gls{dcf} and $30\%$ fewer Voice-attributed collision losses. The cost is about sixteen thousand explicitly dropped Best Effort frames per run.
With the same overlay but sparser alert cadences (\texttt{medium}/\texttt{low}), the improvement disappears, so two conditions must hold together: (i)~the near-crash load is concentrated, and (ii)~the bursts are frequent enough to keep protection active for most of the crash window.

The uniform Poisson matrix provides the reference case.
Under heavy density and high offered load, Voice 95th-percentile alert age is about 2.2~ms under every policy: EDCA does not beat plain \gls{dcf}, and emergency's 2.21~ms versus 2.24~ms is not a material improvement of the Voice tail.
The policies differ there in their cost to Best Effort traffic: \texttt{stable} raises fleet Best Effort P95 from 0.51~ms under plain \gls{dcf} to 30~ms; \texttt{guarded} to 3.9~ms; emergency keeps 0.37~ms by dropping competing frames (about $2.5\times10^{3}$ Best Effort drops while blocked)~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
The light-density high-load results show the same pattern with fewer vehicles: the bounded adaptive profiles leave Voice unchanged while inflating Best Effort P95.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:conc:takeaway}

The practical implications therefore depend on the load pattern.
Explicit DSCP-to-Voice mapping remains a reasonable default for crash-marked alerts~\cite{IEEE80211e_2005}.
Adaptive Best Effort suppression, especially emergency preemption, should be enabled as an escalation step only when there is genuine congestion and genuine alert activity, and it should not permanently replace ordinary EDCA.
Deployment would additionally require authentication, rate limiting, and plausibility checks before overheard Voice frames may trigger suppression, because the same local trigger that protects a crash can be abused by faulty or malicious Voice traffic (\autoref{chap:evaluation}).

Under a uniform Poisson offer the absolute Voice delays remain far below representative platooning latency budgets such as the~\SI{25}{\milli\second} end-to-end bound in \gls{threegpp} TS~22.186~\cite{ts22186}, and most of the small remaining delay is the alert generator's own dispatch jitter rather than channel access (\autoref{sec:eval:light}). Under that offer, the risk is therefore the Best Effort cost and not alert latency.
Under concentrated hotspot contention the same budget becomes meaningful: plain \gls{dcf}/EDCA reach Voice P95 of 18.5--20~ms, within reach of that bound, while emergency keeps it at 14.6~ms (\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}).
The contribution is therefore a \gls{mac}-level accounting of protection and cost that depends on the load pattern, with only a cost under diffuse load and a Voice benefit under concentrated load. It is not a claim that the simulated scenario alone guarantees application-level crash outcomes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conc:limits}

The study's limits are those already argued in the Evaluation discussion (\autoref{sec:eval:discussion}); they are restated here as the boundary conditions on the Conclusion.

\noindent\textbf{Simulation scope and statistical strength.}
The evaluation is simulation-only, with no testbed or hardware validation, so claims remain tied to the configured Veins/\gls{inet} model~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.
Heavy-density \glspl{kpi} average three seeds and report the min--max range in the main delay tables, still without confidence intervals or significance tests, and light-density results use the same three-seed rule; the numbers are therefore qualitative mechanism checks under a controlled matrix, not proven effect sizes.
All observed prioritization is statistical contention shaping, not deterministic real-time communication~\cite{kosekszott2012What}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Traffic abstraction and baselines.}
The traffic model is a synthetic two-class Best Effort / Voice abstraction rather than \gls{cam}/\gls{denm} schedules or richer multi-class ITS stacks, and the high crash-burst profile is a deliberate stress case rather than a standardized alert timetable.
Treating \emph{all} Best Effort as suppressible is a worst-case bound for that abstraction: in a real stack, traffic outside the current crash alert may still be safety relevant, so a binary split can underestimate preemption harm (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:cost}).
No adaptive-EDCA or \gls{dcc} baseline is implemented, so no superiority claim is made against those always-on families~\cite{etsi_dcc_2018,romdhani2003AdaptiveEDCF}.
The three adaptive profiles are fixed design points whose triggers are not individually ablated.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Radio, topology, and delivery model.}
Connectivity uses free-space path loss, a static \gls{snir} threshold, and binary obstacle loss; small-scale fading, shadowing, and graded attenuation are omitted (\autoref{chap:implementation}).
The corridor is a one-hop highway multicast setting with a single crash source: overlapping protection windows from concurrent alerts are not evaluated, alerts are not retransmitted as multi-hop relays across the 5~km road, and only the light/heavy density pair with three offered-load levels is swept, rather than a continuous space of speeds, lane layouts, or channel plans.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Interpretation caveats that affect how results are read.}
The reported 2.2~ms Voice P95 quantifies alert age under a moderate Poisson offer and is dominated by the alert generator's per-copy dispatch jitter rather than by medium access, so it is a floor no \gls{mac} policy could have lowered. These delays remain far below representative platooning budgets~\cite{ts22186} and therefore describe the mechanism rather than an application-level safety benefit. Under hotspot contention the baselines approach that budget (18.5--20~ms P95) and emergency keeps 14.6~ms (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:significance}), so absolute claims hold only for each configuration, while Best Effort penalties from untimely blocking can still be severe in both.
Hotspot streaming nodes are placed by injection order rather than by a received-power map (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:limits}), so hotspot gain magnitudes depend on that geometry; placement sweeps belong to future work.
Under \texttt{plain}, Best Effort and Voice statistics differ because the traffic processes differ, not because the single \gls{dcf} queue differentiates them (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:plain}); only within-class, cross-policy comparisons are controlled.
Pooled Best Effort averages understate spatially concentrated cost near the crash source (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:spatial}), and unauthenticated Voice frames can trigger suppression; this denial-of-service risk is bounded by finite windows, but only authentication and rate limiting can remove it (\autoref{sec:eval:disc:dos}).

Within that scope the comparison remains internally fair: all configurations share the radio stack, mobility, and load matrix, so the reported differences isolate \gls{mac}-policy effects~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}.

The paper's evaluation section, however, reports an earlier measurement campaign and covers only the uniform-load density matrices; \autoref{chap:evaluation} re-runs those campaigns, extends the scenario with the concentrated near-crash load overlay in which crash-adjacent vehicles bulk-upload and give protection a measurable Voice return (\autoref{sec:eval:hotspot}), and deliberately supersedes the paper's numerical results.

Its role in this dissertation is recorded in \autoref{sec:rw:prior}, which presents it as the precursor from which the present study deliberately diverges by dropping gate-control scheduling and synchronization.
  \item \textbf{Simulation artifact.} The implementation, scenario packages, and KPI dashboard underlying \autoref{chap:implementation} and \autoref{chap:evaluation} are archived in the public artifact~\cite{aguiar2026_veins_qos}; \autoref{apend:layout} records its directory layout.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:conc:future}

Several extensions follow directly from the limitations above and from the Evaluation discussion.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Stronger evidence and richer scenarios.}
Larger seed batches with confidence intervals or significance tests would move the study from qualitative mechanism checks toward statistically supported effect sizes.
Richer propagation (capture effect, small-scale fading, shadowing, graded obstacles), continuous sweeps of density and offered load, multi-channel or multi-hop relay settings on long corridors, and standardized \gls{cam}/\gls{denm}-like schedules would test whether the protection-versus-cost frontier holds under more realistic radio and traffic stress.
Connecting Voice delay and loss to an application-level safety deadline, crash-avoidance distance, or other outcome metric would address the practical-significance gap left by the present 2.2~ms Voice tails~\cite{ts22186}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Baselines, parameters, and concurrency.}
Activation-criteria ablations and systematic parameter sweeps would separate which trigger and window settings drive the observed Voice gains.
Adaptive-\gls{edca}, ITS-G5 \gls{dcc}, and sidelink baselines would place the event-triggered profiles against established always-on congestion and prioritization families~\cite{etsi_dcc_2018,romdhani2003AdaptiveEDCF}.
Concurrent alert sources and the interaction of their overlapping protection windows remain unevaluated.
A closed-loop activation criterion based on the blocked duty cycle (alert cadence times bounded block window, \autoref{sec:eval:disc:trigger}) would let the controller engage emergency suppression only under the conditions in which \autoref{tab:eval:hotspot_dose} shows a Voice benefit.
Alternative protection timings, including predictive pre-blocking activated from a learned burst cadence, were explored during development but showed no measurable benefit at the sparse-burst settings of this study and are not reported as a controlled ablation; timing remains an open question under concentrated load.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Deployment constraints.}
Security against malicious or faulty Voice triggering (authentication, rate limiting, plausibility checks), finer-grained classes than a binary Voice/Best Effort split, and per-vehicle Best Effort fairness around the crash source are prerequisites before comparable \gls{mac} aggression could be defended outside a synthetic two-class study.
Hardware-in-the-loop or testbed validation would further bound how much of the simulated protection-versus-cost frontier transfers to real 802.11p stacks.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Consolidated journal publication.}
Because the SBESC 2026 paper reports only the earlier, uniform-load measurement campaign (\autoref{sec:conc:publications}), a journal-length follow-up consolidating the full results of this dissertation is planned for the future: the re-run uniform-load matrices, the concentrated near-crash hotspot overlay, and the five-policy protection-versus-cost frontier of \autoref{chap:evaluation}~\cite{aguiar2026crash}.

In summary, crash-aware prioritization in decentralized vehicular Wi-Fi can measurably improve Voice delay and Voice-frame integrity where near-crash congestion is concentrated and bursts are dense enough to keep protection active, but only as a carefully bounded, event-triggered escalation whose cost to ordinary traffic must be acknowledged and constrained.
